THE TAKEAWAY / KEY POINTS

Bring pilot transfer arrangements into traceable fleet management.

  1. The management scope covers the complete Pilot Transfer Arrangement. Pilot ladders, manropes, combination arrangements, strong points, points of access and related equipment need to be inventoried together.
  2. The 2028 management requirements are not the same as the hardware transition deadlines for existing arrangements. Do not wait until the relevant 2029 survey to start inspections, maintenance and familiarization.
  3. Inspect before and after each use, and carry out a detailed inspection every three months. This is done by the onboard Responsible Officer; the frequency does not mean a Class/RO surveyor must attend quarterly.
  4. 36 months from manufacture, or 30 months from being placed into service, whichever comes first. Pilot ladders and manropes, including spares, must be withdrawn earlier if they no longer meet the standard.

Based on SOLAS V/23 as amended by MSC.572(110); MSC.576(110) §§25–30.[1][2]

01

From one ladder to the complete arrangement

The complete arrangement

As a surveyor, the point I most want owners to take is this: replacing a ladder is only one step. The company needs to put the condition, inspection, maintenance and familiarization of the complete pilot transfer arrangement into a management system that can actually be run.

IMO adopted MSC.572(110) on 26 June 2025, amending SOLAS V/23, and MSC.576(110) as the performance standards for pilot transfer arrangements. Under the amendment’s acceptance and entry-into-force procedure, implementation is expected from 1 January 2028.[1][2]

Transfer equipment

Pilot ladder, manropes, and, where applicable, the accommodation ladder, embarkation platform and combination arrangement.

Load-bearing and securing

Strong points, securing ropes, shackles, securing fittings, and pilot ladder winch reels.

Safe access and associated fittings

Point of access, stanchions, handholds, lighting and chafe protection.

Ongoing management

Inspection, stowage, maintenance, identification, replacement and familiarization — covering both in-use and spare equipment.

What already existsExisting requirements already cover equipment safety, inspection and some records. The HSSC 2025 Safety Equipment Survey already covers arrangement, access, lighting, operation, and records of when the pilot ladder was placed into service and of repairs. The new rules make frequency, the maintenance plan and withdrawal limits more specific. They should not be read as “nothing was managed before.”[3]

Practical suggestion: Build the requirements into the PMS (Planned Maintenance System) and SMS (Safety Management System), and assign ship and shore responsibilities for maintenance and tracking.

02

Separate the two application timelines first

Application & transition

Start with two questions: when was the arrangement installed, and does SOLAS Chapter I apply to the ship? The transition for design and arrangement cannot be applied wholesale to day-to-day management requirements.[1]

2026–2027 Preparation advice

Complete a Fleet Gap Analysis and start preparing the fleet.

Inventory equipment, collect dates and manufacturer documents, and set up PMS jobs, a maintenance plan, procurement and training. This is preparation advice in this note, not a separate statutory deadline.

2028.01.01 Expected entry into force

All arrangements start meeting the management requirements; new installations follow the new standards.

Regardless of installation date, inspection, stowage, maintenance, replacement and familiarization apply under the Introduction and Parts D–E. Arrangements installed on or after that date: design, manufacture, arrangement and rigging apply under the Introduction and Parts A–C, with applicable approval under Part F.

From 2029.01.01 Existing arrangements

Ships to which Chapter I applies: comply no later than the first survey on or after that date.

For arrangements installed before 2028, comply with the amended V/23.3 no later than this survey. The fleet and the flag State / RO should confirm ship-by-ship survey and upgrade arrangements early.

2030.01.01 Another application branch

Ships to which Chapter I does not apply: latest compliance date for existing arrangements.

Ships within the scope of the amended V/23 but not subject to Chapter I have this separate deadline; do not apply the 2029 first-survey date across the board.

Do not substitute the ship’s year of build or the date crew rigged the ladder.[1]
03

Put inspection into every operation

Inspection & onboard workflow

MSC.576(110) §25 distinguishes two frequencies: inspection before and after each use, and a detailed inspection every three months; both are carried out by the onboard Responsible Officer. Introduction §3.7 also requires a responsible officer to be designated to supervise inspection, rigging and the transfer of personnel.[2]

A complete embarkation and disembarkation sequence

  1. STEP 01Pre-use inspectionBefore use
  2. STEP 02Correct riggingRigging
  3. STEP 03Personnel transferPilot transfer
  4. STEP 04Post-use inspectionAfter use
  5. STEP 05Correct stowageStowage

This flow is a working sketch based on the inspection requirements; after rigging, the complete arrangement must also be confirmed as safe to use. Shipboard procedures should specify the operating sequence and inspection scope in accordance with the applicable requirements and the manufacturer's instructions.

What to check before and after use?

  • Ladder and ropes: condition of side ropes, steps, spreaders and manropes, plus wear, damage and contamination.
  • Securing and load-bearing: securing arrangements, strong points, shackles and possible chafing.
  • Access and operation: point of access, stanchions／handholds, lighting, rigging, and, where applicable, the combination arrangement.

Post-use inspection can catch damage from that operation, so defects can be dealt with before the next transfer. After the job, follow through with inspection, any necessary action, and correct stowage.

Every three months, inspect the complete arrangement in detail

In a statutory survey, the Class／RO surveyor checks equipment and the management system against applicable requirements; “inspect every three months” does not itself mean a surveyor must attend quarterly.
04

The maintenance plan must be available — and usable

Maintenance plan & records

MSC.576(110) §27 requires a Maintenance Plan that can be presented for inspection. It should be easy to understand, with illustrations where appropriate, and include at least these six parts.[2]

01 · Checklists

Cover pre- and post-use and three-monthly inspection work.

02 · Maintenance, repair and stowage instructions

Follow the manufacturer’s instructions and spell out what may and may not be done.

03 · Periodic work schedule

List the timetable for periodic inspection and maintenance.

04 · Spares and replacement sources

Keep sources from which suitable spare parts or replacements can be obtained.

05 · Inspection and maintenance records

Keep an inspection／maintenance log traceable to each item of equipment.

06 · In-service and anticipated withdrawal dates

Track the in-service date of pilot ladders and manropes, and the anticipated withdrawal date calculated under §30.

For each item, build a traceable data chain

Equipment ID／Serial No. → Manufacture Date → In-Service Date → Anticipated Withdrawal Date → inspection and maintenance records.

Manufacture date should be confirmed from the equipment marking and documents; the onboard receipt date is also worth keeping for procurement and delivery tracking. Delivered onboard is not the same as Placed into service — manage the two dates in separate fields.

Beyond “the ladder still looks all right”,
be able to answer: when was it manufactured, when was it placed into service, and when must it be withdrawn?

A surveyor’s view · Tie equipment condition back to auditable records

Include familiarization in joining procedures

Part E requires seafarers involved in inspection, maintenance, rigging or operation to receive familiarization matching their duties. For ships to which SOLAS Chapter IX applies, the company must ensure, under the relevant requirements, that seafarers are familiar with the ship’s arrangements and safe operation; for ships to which Chapter IX does not apply, see §33.[2]

Suggested training content: correct rigging, pre- and post-use inspection, three-monthly detailed inspection, defect and withdrawal judgement, spare replacement, combination arrangements, and operational considerations in adverse weather. This can be built into joining familiarization, Deck Officer familiarization and SMS training.

05

36／30 months change procurement and inventory logic

Life-cycle & procurement

Under MSC.576(110) §30, pilot ladders and manropes, including spares, must be withdrawn from use for the embarkation or disembarkation of pilots or other personnel at whichever of the following occurs first.[2]

From the date of manufacture
36months

Manufacture date + 36 months

From the date placed into service
30months

In-service date + 30 months

Whichever limit comes first; if the equipment first fails to meet the performance standards, withdraw it then.
Dates are time limits, not a guarantee of fitness until that day.

Case: delayed placing into service is still limited by manufacture date

Suppose a ladder is manufactured in early 2028, delivered onboard in September, and only placed into service in October. Procurement and onboard records need to calculate both date limits from the start.

A note for procurementUnused stock still consumes the 36-month limit from manufacture. At purchase, watch manufacture date, pre-delivery storage time and expected in-service date, so a large stockpile does not leave too little remaining life.

For spares not yet placed into service, do not invent an in-service date. Track manufacture date, condition and stowage first; log the in-service date when the item is actually placed into service, then re-compare the two time limits.

06

Spares, repairs and stowage need managing too

Spares, repairs & stowage

A spare ladder in the store is still in scope

§29 requires at least one compliant spare pilot ladder and one set of compliant spare manropes. Spares must also be brought into identification, inspection, maintenance, stowage and the §30 withdrawal control.[2]

Clear limits on repairing steps and spreaders

§28 prohibits repair or replacement of pilot ladder steps or spreader steps. When damage requires a step to be replaced, the ladder should not be kept in use after swapping a board and re-seizing the ropes onboard.

Other repairs go back to the manufacturer’s instructions

Repairs to rope seizings or securing devices are judged against the instructions provided under §24 and the list of permitted onboard repairs; do not expand that list on your own.

Ask the manufacturer for complete use and maintenance documents

Besides the Product Certificate, obtain inspection, maintenance, repair, stowage and discard criteria. §24 covers pre- and post-use inspection, detailed periodic inspection, permitted onboard repairs, securing methods, and written and illustrated descriptions of damage or condition requiring withdrawal.[2]

Stowage conditions are part of inspection

§23 requires equipment, when not in use, to be stowed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to prevent deterioration from moisture, icing, sunlight, chemicals, grease and similar contaminants. Prolonged exposure to weather or oily conditions will make later inspections need to look further into how the equipment was stored and the condition of the ropes.

Practical approach: List spare ladders and spare manropes item by item in the PMS inventory, clearly showing location, ID, dates, last inspection and next job, so spares truly remain ready to replace.

07

Seven things owners can start now

Owner action plan

Fleet preparation can start with these seven items. Completing an initial inventory in 2026–2027 is this article’s advice, to spread the workload of equipment upgrades, document updates and crew training.

  1. Inventory each ship’s arrangement and build an equipment register

    Build a fleet inventory covering pilot ladders, manropes, spares, securing arrangements and related fittings; keep serial／ID, manufacture, delivery, in-service and anticipated withdrawal dates.

    Ship-by-ship Pilot Transfer Arrangement Register

  2. Review existing arrangements and confirm upgrade and approval needs

    Use the Introduction and Parts A–C to identify gaps, and Part F to confirm submissions and approvals; connect flag State／RO comments, modification budgets and survey arrangements.

    Fleet Gap Analysis + upgrade and survey plan

  3. Set up PMS jobs and a Maintenance Plan

    Include pre- and post-use inspection, three-monthly detailed inspection, maintenance, defect handling and records, so each job maps to the correct equipment.

    PMS job items + ship maintenance plan + record forms

  4. Collect manufacturer documents and confirm repair and discard criteria

    Centralise certificates, identification markings, inspection instructions, permitted onboard repairs, stowage methods and discard criteria.

    An equipment document pack that ship and shore can find

  5. Adjust procurement and spare arrangements

    Confirm a compliant spare ladder and spare manropes; at purchase, check applicable type approval, manufacture date, delivery time and remaining service life, and include ongoing replacement in the budget.

    Procurement specification + spare list + withdrawal schedule

  6. Update the SMS and crew familiarization

    Update the Pilot Boarding Procedure, Responsible Officer designation and ship–shore split of duties; include inspection, rigging, defect judgement, replacement and adverse-weather considerations in familiarization.

    Operating procedures + duty split + familiarization records

  7. Audit implementation once before the deadline

    Review implementation across equipment, documents and people, and track gaps to close-out; confirm ship-by-ship application dates, survey arrangements and when necessary upgrades will be complete.

    A trackable gap list and completion evidence

09

Four easy-to-mix-up questions

Common questions

Can an existing ship wait until the 2029 survey before starting three-monthly inspections?

Do not treat the two as the same thing. Amended V/23.6 applies inspection, stowage, maintenance, replacement and familiarization to all arrangements regardless of installation date; hardware transition for existing arrangements is judged separately under V/23.4 or V/23.5.[1]

Does a three-monthly inspection mean Class／RO must carry it out every quarter?

The detailed inspection in §25 is carried out by the onboard Responsible Officer. The surveyor’s statutory survey and the ship’s periodic inspections are different jobs; keep the responsibilities and records distinct.[2]

If a spare ladder has never been used, does it have no service-life limit?

It is still subject to the 36-month limit from manufacture and to equipment condition; after it is placed into service, also compare the 30-month limit. §30 expressly covers spare pilot ladders and spare manropes.[2]

If the ladder is in good condition, can it be used beyond 36／30 months?

“Good condition” cannot extend the §30 time limits; if the performance standards are not met, it may have to be withdrawn even earlier. Time and condition are both control criteria.[2]

REAY'S PERSPECTIVE / SURVEYOR'S NOTE

Let every safe transfer rest on management behind it.

MSC.576(110) brings together approval and arrangement, day-to-day inspection, maintenance, crew familiarization and eventual replacement as a traceable set of requirements. For owners, the outcome of preparation should be this: every ladder, set of manropes and related fittings has someone responsible, records that can be checked, and a clear withdrawal plan.
